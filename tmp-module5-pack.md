## Public module overview

People often seek mental health support through several sources, including family, elders, peers, faith communities, traditional practitioners, primary care, counselors, psychologists, social workers, and doctors.

This module examines how spiritual, traditional, community, and professional supports can work in a connected care pathway without being treated as identical. Learners explore meaning, belonging, evidence, medicine safety, rights, consent, role boundaries, referral, and the unequal histories that shape trust.

The goal is not to force a choice between culture and science. It is to build culturally respectful coordination that preserves voluntary choice, protects rights, makes evidence limits clear, and prevents urgent or effective care from being delayed.

## Module competency

By the end of this module, learners should be able to design a culturally respectful partnership among spiritual, traditional, community, and professional support systems with clear roles, informed consent, human rights protections, medicine safety, warm referral, follow up, and lived experience accountability.

## Learning objectives

1. Map the distinct roles that faith leaders, spiritual communities, traditional practitioners, peers, families, and mental health professionals may hold.
2. Explain how prayer, ritual, communal gathering, meaning making, traditional practices, and clinical care may support different needs.
3. Assess strengths and risks without romanticizing cultural support or treating professional care as culture free.
4. Analyze trust, colonial history, regulation, evidence, power, consent, privacy, safeguarding, and medicine safety in a partnership.
5. Design a culturally respectful coordination model with role boundaries, warm referral, follow up, rights protections, and lived experience accountability.

## Original Tamu Academy introduction

People rarely enter mental health support through one doorway.

A person may speak first with a relative, elder, pastor, imam, traditional practitioner, peer, teacher, nurse, counselor, psychologist, social worker, or doctor. They may use several supports at the same time. This reality is often called plural care.

Plural care can provide meaning, belonging, practical help, culturally familiar language, and earlier contact. It can also become fragmented or unsafe when providers dismiss one another, roles are unclear, information is shared without consent, untested claims are treated as certainty, harmful practices are hidden as culture, or urgent care is delayed.

This module does not ask learners to choose between culture and science. It asks how different supports can relate honestly, protect rights, respect a person's beliefs and choices, disclose evidence limits, and make timely referral possible.

## Original explanation

### 1. More than one doorway to care

A care pathway is the sequence of people, places, explanations, and decisions through which someone seeks support.

The pathway may be:

1. **Sequential:** A person tries one support, then another.
2. **Concurrent:** A person uses several supports at the same time.
3. **Parallel but disconnected:** Several providers are involved but do not know what the others are doing.
4. **Coordinated:** Distinct providers communicate with consent, understand their roles, refer, and follow up.

Using more than one support is not automatically safe or unsafe. Ask:

1. What does each support actually do?
2. What evidence and risks apply?
3. Is participation voluntary?
4. Is the support accessible and respectful?
5. Does the pathway protect timely access to appropriate care?

### 2. Faith, spirituality, and traditional healing are not one category

Religious communities, spiritual practices, and traditional healing systems vary across countries, peoples, generations, denominations, lineages, and individual practitioners. Some roles overlap. Others disagree strongly with one another.

Possible contributions include:

1. **Faith leader or congregation:** meaning, prayer, belonging, practical aid, grief support, moral language, recognition, and referral.
2. **Traditional practitioner:** cultural interpretation, ritual, herbal knowledge, family or ancestral meaning, community trust, recognition, and referral.
3. **Peer, elder, or family network:** companionship, practical help, advocacy, daily support, and recognition.
4. **Clinical or social care professional:** assessment, treatment, rehabilitation, protection, social support, medicine safety, and referral.

These are not universal job descriptions. A traditional practitioner may specialize in herbs, divination, ritual, counseling, birth, bones, or other practices. A faith leader may or may not provide counseling. A clinician may also hold religious or traditional beliefs.

### 3. What spiritual and traditional supports may provide

Spiritual or traditional support may offer:

1. A language for suffering, hope, identity, grief, duty, and repair
2. Belonging and recognition within a trusted community
3. Rituals that mark transition, loss, reconciliation, or renewed purpose
4. Practical help with food, transport, caregiving, shelter, or social connection
5. A first conversation where professional services are scarce or stigmatized
6. Continuity with family, history, land, ancestors, faith, or moral commitments
7. Voluntary support alongside professional care

A practice may feel meaningful or supportive without being an evidence based treatment for a specific condition. Testimony, cultural value, safety, and clinical effectiveness answer different questions.

### 4. Where harm can occur

Risks include:

1. **Delay:** Necessary care is postponed until a ritual or prayer period ends.
2. **Blame and stigma:** Distress is framed as weak faith, sin, curse, or personal failure.
3. **Coercion:** Forced prayer, ritual, confinement, restraint, fasting, confession, or treatment.
4. **Unsafe products:** Unknown ingredients, dose, contamination, toxicity, or interaction with medicine.
5. **Privacy failure:** Personal information is shared with family or a congregation without consent.
6. **Exploitation:** Fear is used to demand money, loyalty, secrecy, or repeated services.

These risks are not unique to spiritual or traditional settings. Clinical services can also violate rights, use coercion, dismiss culture, misread distress, create financial burden, or provide inaccessible and fragmented care.

One rights standard must apply across the entire pathway.

### 5. Professional care is not culture free

Professional knowledge is valuable, but professional institutions are shaped by language, training, law, history, resources, and power.

Colonial systems often marginalized African knowledge while building unequal services. That history can affect current trust. Naming it does not make every traditional practice safe or every clinical practice harmful.

Culturally humble care should:

1. Ask how the person names and understands the concern.
2. Ask which supports the person values or fears.
3. Explain professional reasoning and uncertainty plainly.
4. Offer options and preserve decision making.
5. Check language, access, cost, disability, gender, and safety.
6. Review outcomes and harms with lived experience groups.

### 6. A rights floor for every setting

Every partnership requires:

1. Dignity and non discrimination
2. Voluntary participation and meaningful informed consent
3. The ability to ask questions, refuse, stop, or choose another support
4. Freedom from violence, humiliation, exploitation, forced confession, unsafe restraint, and abusive confinement
5. Privacy with clearly explained legal and safety limits
6. Safeguarding for children and adults at risk
7. Timely access to appropriate health, social, protection, and urgent services
8. Accessible complaints, independent review, and protection from retaliation
9. Participation by people with lived experience in design, oversight, and evaluation

A practice does not become acceptable because it is described as traditional, spiritual, clinical, familial, or communal.

### 7. Clear roles make respect possible

**Faith or spiritual leader**

May offer voluntary spiritual care, belonging, practical support, education, recognition, and referral.

Must not claim clinical diagnosis or treatment without the required qualification and scope.

**Traditional practitioner**

May provide a defined cultural or traditional practice, transparent product information, recognition, and referral.

Must not use coercion, abuse, secret medicine combinations, or delay necessary care.

**Clinician**

May assess, treat, review medicines, plan safety, and refer within professional scope.

Must not ridicule beliefs, require cultural abandonment, or claim that professional care is culturally neutral.

**Youth or lived experience advisor**

May guide governance, language, access, accountability, and evaluation.

Must not become an unpaid substitute for clinical, safeguarding, or emergency staff.

When one person holds several identities, the active role, duty, confidentiality rule, and scope must be stated.

### 8. Referral is a relationship

A warm referral should:

1. Recognize a need or request beyond the current role.
2. Explain the concern and options in clear language.
3. Ask what support the person prefers.
4. Obtain consent for coordination.
5. Identify an available and accessible destination.
6. Make the connection through a named contact, appointment, transport, or accompanied handoff when wanted.
7. Share only necessary information.
8. Follow up on whether the connection worked.
9. Use the urgent response pathway immediately when delay creates danger.

### 9. Medicine and herbal product safety

Herbal preparations may contain active ingredients. The identity, concentration, dose, contamination risk, and interaction evidence may be clear for some products and uncertain for others.

Safe coordination should:

1. Ask about prescribed medicines, over the counter products, herbs, supplements, fasting, and other practices without ridicule.
2. Record product name, ingredients, source, dose, schedule, and reason for use when known and consented.
3. Use a qualified clinician or pharmacist to review possible interactions and health risks.
4. Encourage both providers to explain limits and warning signs.
5. Avoid sudden treatment changes without qualified guidance.
6. Create a pathway for reporting possible adverse effects and unsafe products.

### 10. Ghana system case

Ghana's WHO Assessment Instrument for Mental Health Systems report describes a plural system in which people often visit traditional or faith based facilities before or during professional treatment.

The report:

1. Identified 1,705 traditional and faith based facilities.
2. Described national guidelines intended to protect dignity and rights.
3. Identified prohibitions on chaining, caging, forced confession, flogging, starvation, and forced marriage.
4. Described collaboration, training, and interest among some practitioners in professional support.
5. Also reported continued abuse, weak records, limited evidence for treatment claims, and insufficient regulatory resources.

Ghana is useful as a governance case. It is not proof that collaboration is complete or uniformly safe.

### 11. Rural Uganda and unequal trust

A 2025 qualitative study in Buyende District interviewed traditional healers, faith healers, and primary health care providers and then held focus groups.

The study:

1. Treated the three provider groups as distinct.
2. Found willingness among traditional and faith healers to collaborate with professional providers.
3. Found uneven trust and negative views among some groups.
4. Discussed how colonial and postcolonial power can shape which knowledge systems are treated as legitimate.

The study helps explain experience, meaning, trust, referral, and possible collaboration barriers in one district. It does not measure treatment effectiveness or represent all of Uganda.

### 12. What collaboration can include

A 2024 scoping review found documented collaboration activities such as:

1. Relationship building
2. Training across practitioner groups
3. Coordinated meetings
4. Cross referral
5. Treatment plan discussion
6. Joint health promotion

The review also found that documented bidirectional service collaborations were limited and located within professional health facilities.

Partnership should include:

1. Time and mutual recognition
2. Two way learning
3. Defined referral criteria and real destinations
4. Consent and minimum necessary information sharing
5. Accurate public claims
6. Community and lived experience authority

## Interactive scenario

### Title

Mwangaza Care Partnership

### Scenario introduction

A fictional district plans a youth mental health partnership among a health center, churches, mosques, traditional practitioners, a disability organization, and a youth council.

The first draft asks spiritual and traditional leaders to identify mental disorders, requires thirty days of prayer or ritual before clinical referral, places personal stories in a shared messaging group, and gives the youth council no decision authority.

No one has agreed on urgent response, medicine safety, complaints, or follow up.

### Scenario instructions

> Make six decisions. Each decision will show educational feedback. No decision diagnoses a person or decides whether a spiritual belief is true. The goal is to protect choice, rights, safety, honest role boundaries, and continuity of support.

### Decision 1: Entry and choice

**Prompt:** How should a person enter the partnership?

**Options:**

1. Require every participant to begin with the provider selected by the partnership chair.
2. Offer several entry points, ask what support the person wants, explain roles and limits, and permit voluntary combinations or changes.
3. Require professional care first so cultural beliefs do not affect the pathway.
4. Require spiritual or traditional care first to prove community respect.

**Preferred option index:** 1

**Feedback:**

1. This removes meaningful choice and may concentrate power in one role.
2. This centers the person, preserves several safe entry points, and makes roles visible.
3. Professional care may be valuable, but mandatory sequencing can erase preference and access realities.
4. Cultural respect does not justify a fixed waiting period or required practice.

### Decision 2: Partner roles

**Prompt:** Which role structure is most appropriate?

**Options:**

1. Every provider may diagnose and treat after one joint workshop.
2. Only clinicians may speak about mental health, while community partners recruit participants.
3. Each partner has a written scope, mutual training, supervision and referral duties, and no one claims competence they do not hold.
4. Role boundaries are unnecessary when providers trust one another.

**Preferred option index:** 2

**Feedback:**

1. A workshop does not create every required qualification or legal scope.
2. This uses community partners instrumentally and ignores the support they may legitimately provide.
3. This preserves distinct contributions while protecting role clarity and safe escalation.
4. Trust is valuable, but it does not replace competence, accountability, or scope.

### Decision 3: Routine and urgent referral

**Prompt:** When should referral occur?

**Options:**

1. Only after thirty days of prayer or ritual.
2. Only when the current provider feels personally unable to help.
3. According to written role based criteria, with immediate use of the established urgent pathway when delay creates danger.
4. At the end of every encounter, regardless of need or preference.

**Preferred option index:** 2

**Feedback:**

1. A fixed waiting period can delay necessary or urgent care.
2. Referral should not depend only on confidence or personal judgment without agreed criteria.
3. This connects ordinary role limits with a pathway that does not delay urgent action.
4. Automatic referral may ignore preference, overload services, and weaken meaningful coordination.

### Decision 4: Medicine and herbal product safety

**Prompt:** How should the partnership handle concurrent use?

**Options:**

1. Tell participants to hide traditional products from clinicians.
2. Ban every herbal preparation without asking what it is.
3. Encourage open disclosure without ridicule, identify ingredients and doses when possible, obtain qualified interaction review, and monitor possible harm.
4. Assume trusted providers cannot prescribe products that interact.

**Preferred option index:** 2

**Feedback:**

1. Secrecy can prevent identification of interactions or adverse effects.
2. Blanket dismissal can damage trust and does not produce a specific safety assessment.
3. This combines respect with transparent and qualified safety review.
4. Trust does not determine chemistry, contamination, dose, or interaction risk.

### Decision 5: Information sharing

**Prompt:** What information rule should apply?

**Options:**

1. Share full stories in a partnership messaging group so everyone is informed.
2. Share only the minimum necessary information through a secure process with clear consent and explained legal or safety limits.
3. Never share information, even when the person asks for a coordinated handoff.
4. Let each provider decide their own privacy rule.

**Preferred option index:** 1

**Feedback:**

1. A shared messaging group can expose highly sensitive information and exceeds what most partners need.
2. This supports coordination while protecting choice, privacy, and role based access.
3. Absolute non sharing can block a person requested referral and continuity.
4. A partnership needs a shared, lawful, and understandable privacy standard.

### Decision 6: Governance and evaluation

**Prompt:** How should the partnership be governed and assessed?

**Options:**

1. Let the largest institution make decisions and ask youth to share stories at public events.
2. Count referrals and attendance only.
3. Give youth, disability, and lived experience members real decision authority and compensation, protect stories, and measure access, respect, referral, safety, rights, equity, and provider support.
4. Avoid complaints or adverse event measures because they may harm the partnership's reputation.

**Preferred option index:** 2

**Feedback:**

1. Visibility without authority or story protection is token participation.
2. Activity counts do not show whether people had choice, reached care, or experienced harm.
3. This connects participation with authority and uses measures that match the partnership's responsibilities.
4. Hiding problems prevents correction and increases risk.

### Scenario summary logic

After all six decisions, display:

1. The learner's selected option for each decision
2. The educational feedback for that option
3. A summary across six areas:
   1. Choice
   2. Role clarity
   3. Referral
   4. Medicine safety
   5. Privacy
   6. Governance and accountability
4. One sentence identifying the strongest design feature
5. One sentence identifying the most important revision

Do not label scenario options as a clinical score. Do not store responses publicly.

## Applied activity

### Title

PATHWAYS Partnership and Referral Lab

### Purpose

Learners design a fictional partnership charter, referral map, and ninety day preparation plan.

### PATHWAYS framework

1. **P: Person, preference, and purpose**
   1. Define the coordination problem.
   2. Write respectful questions about beliefs, desired support, language, and prior experiences.
   3. State which supports a person may accept, combine, refuse, pause, or change.
2. **A: Authority, access, and accountability**
   1. Define who decides partnership rules, training, referral, data, complaints, budgets, and public claims.
   2. Address cost, transport, wait time, disability, language, gender, age, privacy, and digital access.
3. **T: Tasks, training, and boundaries**
   1. Define allowed and prohibited tasks for every partner.
   2. State how role switching is disclosed.
   3. Build mutual training, competency, supervision, compensation, and worker support.
4. **H: Human rights and harm prevention**
   1. Create rules for consent, non coercion, privacy, safeguarding, refusal, complaints, and timely care.
   2. Name prohibited practices.
   3. Set a stop rule.
5. **W: Warm referral and follow up**
   1. Map routine professional, social, protection, medicine safety, urgent, and return referrals.
   2. Define follow up and referral failure responses.
6. **A: Awareness, exchange, and cultural humility**
   1. Plan two way learning about local meaning, history, evidence, spiritual diversity, professional culture, and public communication.
7. **Y: Youth and lived experience voice**
   1. Give decision rights, compensation, accessibility, privacy, and story protection.
8. **S: Safety, sustainability, and study**
   1. Select measures for access, choice, respect, trust, referral, delay, rights, medicine safety, equity, and provider workload.
   2. Create financing and evidence statements.

### Warm referral practice

Use this fictional situation:

> Imani tells a trusted community leader that sleep and concentration have worsened, they sometimes feel unsafe, and they want prayer while also considering professional care. Imani uses an herbal preparation and prescribed medicine but has not told either provider.

Learners write five short parts:

1. Listen and affirm without blame or certainty about cause.
2. Explain the current provider's role and limits.
3. Use the established urgent pathway without delay.
4. Coordinate chosen spiritual support, professional care, and medicine review through consent.
5. Explain follow up.

### Question 1

**Prompt:** What does plural care mean in this module?

**Options:**

1. Every community uses the same spiritual explanation for distress.
2. A person may use more than one source of support, whether those sources are coordinated or not.
3. Traditional healing and faith healing are interchangeable.
4. Professional care should replace every cultural or spiritual practice.

**Correct index:** 1

**Feedback:** Plural care describes the reality that people may seek support from family, faith communities, traditional practitioners, peers, primary care, and mental health professionals in sequence or at the same time. These supports should not be treated as identical.

### Question 2

**Prompt:** A learner says that a relative understands distress through a spiritual explanation. What is the most culturally humble response from a care professional?

**Options:**

1. Ridicule the explanation so the person accepts clinical language.
2. Agree that the spiritual explanation is medically proven.
3. Ask what the explanation means to the person, assess needs and safety, discuss options, and respect chosen spiritual support when it is voluntary and does not delay necessary care.
4. Avoid discussing the belief because culture is outside professional care.

**Correct index:** 2

**Feedback:** Cultural humility requires curiosity, respect, and honest limits. A professional need not dismiss a belief or present it as clinical fact. The person's goals, consent, safety, and access to appropriate care remain central.

### Question 3

**Prompt:** Which partnership design best protects both cultural meaning and safe care?

**Options:**

1. A faith leader diagnoses conditions while the clinician approves every spiritual practice.
2. Distinct roles, mutual learning, consent based information sharing, warm referral, rights safeguards, qualified supervision, and community and lived experience accountability.
3. All providers use one explanation of distress and one treatment plan.
4. Referral occurs only after spiritual or traditional methods have failed for a fixed period.

**Correct index:** 1

**Feedback:** Coordination does not require every provider to perform the same task or share the same explanation. It requires clear boundaries, respect, consent, referral, follow up, rights protection, and accountability.

### Question 4

**Prompt:** During a support meeting, a person says they may be in immediate danger. What should happen first?

**Options:**

1. Keep the statement secret because spiritual conversations are always confidential.
2. Complete a ritual before deciding whether other help is needed.
3. Ask an untrained volunteer to determine a diagnosis.
4. Follow the established local urgent response and safeguarding pathway without delay, while allowing chosen spiritual support to continue when it is safe and does not interfere with urgent care.

**Correct index:** 3

**Feedback:** Immediate danger requires the established urgent response. No spiritual, traditional, peer, or educational support should delay qualified assessment or emergency action. Voluntary support may accompany care when safe.

### Question 5

**Prompt:** A participant uses an herbal preparation and also takes prescribed medicine. What is the safest guidance?

**Options:**

1. Stop the prescribed medicine immediately because natural products cannot interact with it.
2. Keep the herbal preparation secret to avoid disrespecting the practitioner.
3. Encourage transparent discussion with a qualified clinician or pharmacist and the traditional practitioner, identify ingredients and doses when possible, review interaction risks, and avoid sudden treatment changes without qualified advice.
4. Assume the combination is safe if both providers are trusted.

**Correct index:** 2

**Feedback:** Herbal products can have active effects, uncertain ingredients, side effects, or interactions with other medicines. Respectful coordination includes accurate disclosure and qualified safety review.

## Module closing

> A bridge is useful only when people can cross it safely in both directions. A partnership that asks traditional or faith leaders only to send people toward professional care is not fully mutual. A partnership that blocks professional care in the name of culture is not safe.
